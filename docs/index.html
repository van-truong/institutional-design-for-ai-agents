Seoul, July 2026

An early version of this work, Multi-Agent AI Systems Need Institutional Design, Not Just Model-Level Alignment, was presented at ICML 2026 in Seoul, South Korea, as a spotlight at the Trustworthy AI for Good Workshop and at the Culture × AI: Evaluating AI as a Cultural Technology Workshop. Thank you to everyone who stopped by to chat with us and for warmly sharing your feedback and ideas.

Our position

Multi-agent AI safety requires intentional institutional design. Even individually aligned agents can produce collectively unsafe outcomes when sharing finite resources. We believe agent systems should be studied as governed systems whose institutions shape collective behavior.

Problem

LLM agents are already embedded in real-world settings.

Every day, teams of sub-agents—on your computer or across the Internet—compete for and share limited tools and resources. Shared codebases, public databases, and compute form an artificial commons, where the old collective-action failures can return.

Aligned individual agents do not simply add up to a cooperative society.

It is already happening

AI agents are overstepping in real systems, not only in the lab.

A running record of documented cases where agents broke rules, broke out, or worked together in ways no one intended. Each one links to its source.

From an individual model to a society of institutions

Model-level evaluation looks only at the center.

We adapt Bronfenbrenner's nested systems. An AI agent sits at the center, inside the groups it works with and the platform, market, and legal institutions that govern it from outside. Influence runs both ways, and every layer changes over time.

The agent in its institutional context

Institutions can be designed or can emerge. For AI agents today, design must lead.

Individual models sit inside agent–group interactions, which sit inside governing institutions. The rings beside each layer match the nested systems above.

Motivational design space

Patterns that shape cooperation appear across human communities.

For centuries, people have tried warnings, sanctions, and shared norms, among other ways of living with common resources. Some serve the greater good, and others risk becoming coercive regimes.

The point is not to copy human institutions wholesale.

It is to notice the range of arrangements people have already tried—and ask what transfers, what fails, and what must be designed differently for agent societies.

Our current framework

Aligning individual agents is not enough. Group-level design is needed.

We start with four cooperation dilemmas that recur wherever resources are shared, plus two more that go beyond them, coordination and collusion. There are likely many, many more worth discussing too!

The design space of agent institutions

Functions say what an institution must do. Mechanism families say how it acts on agents.

Seven institutional functions and six mechanism families. Three of the functions make behavior expected and visible, and four respond to it. Three of the families change the game agents play, and three shape how agents interact over time.

What a designer applies

Mechanism

One specific lever, such as a fine, a reputation score, or a monitor. The taxonomy below maps over 60 of them.

What emerges on its own

Phenomenon

A group-level pattern that comes out of interaction, such as a shared convention or tacit collusion.

Eight more terms
Agent
An LLM-based system that acts with some autonomy on someone’s behalf.
Agent society
Many agents interacting again and again in a shared environment. It implies no human-like mind.
Artificial commons
A shared resource, such as compute, memory, or decision authority, that one agent’s use can wear down for others.
Institution
The rules, roles, and procedures that set what agents may do, who judges a breach, and what follows.
Function and family
A function is a job an institution must do (there are seven). A family groups mechanisms by how they act on agents (there are six).
Sanction
What follows an action, such as a reward, a fine, exclusion, or a duty to repair.
Interaction failure
Harm that comes from how agents relate to one another, not from a flaw in any one of them.
Model-in-institution
A model studied together with its role, how it is watched and sanctioned, and the agents around it.

We mapped what’s in the literature to a unified taxonomy.

We thematically coded 645 documented uses of cooperation-shaping mechanisms, from lab experiments and legal rules to peer-to-peer protocols and LLM-agent studies. 549 of them act through one of 66 mechanisms in 18 themes, grouped by the six mechanism families of the paper; the rest describe group-level phenomena or fall outside the scope. How the counts add up. 256 instances from 185 multi-agent studies (one per mechanism a study tests) + 321 from other fields and systems + 68 seed examples = 645. Of these, 440 single mechanisms + 109 named combinations = 549 appear in the map; the other 82 describe group-level phenomena and 14 fall outside the scope.

Open circles mark mechanisms that no LLM-agent study has tested yet. We keep the gaps visible.

How to read this map

These categories come from interpretive, thematic coding. Choosing the lever as the organizing idea, deciding where codes are merged or split, and naming each category all reflect our reading of the sources. Another team, or another lens, such as grouping mechanisms by discipline or by who holds authority, could draw the boundaries differently, and there is no single correct way to do it. We treat the taxonomy as a working map. Its categories will be merged, split, renamed, and reinterpreted as sources are added. Counts describe our corpus, not how common or effective a mechanism is in the world.

If you would organize a mechanism differently, or know a source we missed, open an issue. Different perspectives are welcome.

Text list of all mechanisms

Mechanisms are what we design. Phenomena are what emerges.

Alongside the mechanisms above, we thematically coded 82 group-level phenomena, patterns that arise from interaction rather than levers a designer applies. The emphasis has shifted with the technology. Multi-agent reinforcement learning mostly documented emergent communication, while LLM-agent studies increasingly report emergent society, such as conventions, social structure, and culture, along with failure modes. Most are neutral or surprising, but 20 are already harmful, almost all in LLM systems and with no institution present.

When cooperation mechanisms fail

Adding a mechanism does not reliably add cooperation.

Three short stories, one for each way a mechanism can backfire. Each follows a rule from the moment it is added to the point where it breaks, then shows a documented case, what to check, and the safeguards.

From a rule change to group outcomes

Every arrow is a place where an institution can fail.

A Coleman boat, adapted from Coleman (1990) and Martínez-Peña and Ylikoski (2024). A change to the rules reaches each agent's situation, shapes what the model does, and only then adds up to outcomes for the group. Designers often hope for the dashed shortcut.

Charting a promising research agenda

It is worth asking: what are the best ways to capture cooperative outcomes?

Capturing cooperation may call for a mix of quantitative and interpretive methods. The practical question is which interaction levels and metrics stay feasible to evaluate as group- and population-level dynamics grow more complex.

Final parting words

So long as humans bear the consequences of AI behavior, we believe humans should have a say in which values are embedded in AI agent institutions.

Even if capable AI systems eventually design and run those institutions themselves, today’s choices may be amplified in future AI agent economies and societies.
